Customer Satisfaction Is Everyone’s Job – So, Who Owns It?

Why the customer’s experience depends on decisions made far beyond Sales.

By Karel Zimmermann · 4 min read

The customer sees one company

Much has been written about customer satisfaction, and I do not claim to have discovered a new principle. But stepping back from the day-to-day demands of running a business has made me reflect on what “owning the customer” meant in practice—and where we sometimes fell short.

“Everyone owns the customer” is easy to say. It is harder to make it meaningful.

A salesperson may make a promise. R&D determines what the product can do. Operations affects its quality and availability. Logistics determines whether it arrives as expected. Finance shapes the terms, and Customer Service responds when something goes wrong.

Each function has its own responsibilities and measures of success. The customer experiences their combined work as one company.

So who makes sure those parts add up to an experience worth coming back for?

Relationships can open the door

Early in my career as a General Manager in the US, I visited customers in New York with one of our salespeople. He had excellent relationships and, with a smile, would tell a customer that it was time to “buy new shoes for his kids”—his way of suggesting that another order would be welcome.

There was warmth and trust in those exchanges. Strong relationships still matter enormously. But a good relationship cannot indefinitely compensate for an incomplete delivery, inconsistent product or problem that no one takes responsibility for resolving.

The promise made in the customer meeting has to survive everything that happens afterward.

When functions improve and the customer loses

At one point, products in a business I worked in reached customers through our distribution platforms. The decision was made to replace each platform’s general manager with two leaders: one responsible for Sales and the other for Logistics. The intention was to improve the efficiency of each function.

Both leaders worked to improve their respective areas. Yet the coordination between them weakened. Deliveries became incomplete, communication with customers became fragmented, and service suffered. We were spending too much time negotiating with one another and too little time looking at the whole experience from the customer’s perspective.

When I later took full responsibility for the overall business, we restored general managers at the platforms. Their role was to bring commercial and operational decisions together, with the capability to run efficient logistics while keeping the customer at the centre. Some business had already been lost, and rebuilding it took time.

The lesson was not that Sales and Logistics should stop improving their own performance. It was that neither could define success solely within its own boundaries. Someone had to be accountable for what the customer received from the platform as a whole.

Make the customer visible inside the business

In a later assignment, I worked with a strong team of functional leaders. Each brought considerable expertise, but we also made a deliberate effort to understand one another’s priorities and their effect on customers.

Every Monday at 2 p.m., we held a one-hour meeting. There was no formal agenda. Each leader briefly shared what had been achieved the previous week and what mattered most in the week ahead. The main focus during these debriefs was primarily on our customers and their experience with our business. Issues requiring joint attention surfaced quickly. These conversations sat alongside our more structured monthly management meetings.

We also brought customer voices into our town halls, dedicating substantial time to their experiences and expectations. It helped people across the business see the connection between their own work and the customer’s reality.

Trust mattered throughout: trust among leaders to address problems together, and trust with customers that we would do what we said. We knew how long it could take to build trust—and how quickly it could be damaged.

Shared responsibility needs clear ownership

Customer satisfaction does belong to everyone who makes a decision affecting the customer. But saying “everyone owns it” cannot mean that no one is responsible for bringing those decisions together.

Leaders have to look beyond the performance of each function and ask what the customer actually experiences. Who sees the complete picture? Who can resolve a problem that crosses departmental lines? And when one function’s improvement makes life harder for the customer, who has the authority to put that right?

Those questions turn customer centricity from an aspiration into a way of running the business.
