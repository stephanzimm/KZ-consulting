Where Strategy meets the Market

Why a clear go-to-market model is essential to turning strategic ambitions into results.

By Karel Zimmermann · 4 min read

Strategy only becomes real in the market. A business strategy may set an ambition, but a clear go-to-market model is what turns that ambition into customers, channels, capabilities—and ultimately revenue.

Strategy only becomes real in the market

Companies devote considerable time and resources to market research, competitor analysis, consumer insights, portfolio reviews and financial targets. Detailed presentations explain where growth will come from and how revenues and margins are expected to develop.

Yet translating those ambitions into execution can remain surprisingly difficult. Strategy development is often treated as an intellectual exercise, with execution expected to follow afterwards. But execution should influence the strategic choices themselves.

A go-to-market model translates strategic choices into customers, channels, value propositions, commercial capabilities—and ultimately revenue.

Without that translation, strategy can remain little more than ambition.

Five questions that make choices clear

A clear go-to-market model should answer five fundamental questions:

  • Which customers are we choosing to serve?
  • What problem are we solving for them?
  • Why should they choose us rather than an alternative?
  • Through which channels and commercial model will we reach them?
  • What capabilities must we build to deliver that promise consistently?

These questions appear straightforward. Answering them clearly—and accepting the consequences of the answers—is considerably more difficult.

From analysis to a real commercial model

In one business I previously led, we developed a new European strategy. The team did an excellent job examining markets, product portfolios, channels, customers and the resources needed to execute the plan. The real breakthrough came when we moved beyond analysis and began making choices.

Individual countries had largely managed their own portfolios and customer priorities. This created complexity and limited our ability to leverage the scale and capabilities of the wider European business.

We standardized a large part of our product and customer/channel priorities through clear product-and-channel matrices. We discontinued parts of the assortment, invested in new products and technologies, and moved from a mainly country-based sales structure to a European channel-based approach. Leaders took responsibility across countries for specific channels and product categories.

These were not simply organizational adjustments. They changed how the business would compete and how resources would be allocated.

Every strategic choice has consequences

The new model placed greater emphasis on innovation, consumer insights and relevance for specific customers and channels. It demanded a different level of coordination between Sales, Marketing and R&D—and, in some roles, different capabilities.

That led to the most difficult part of the transformation: determining whether we had the right people in the right roles. We needed to bring in new capabilities, develop existing team members where possible, and acknowledge that not every role or individual would fit the new model.

Talent decisions are not a secondary HR workstream within strategy execution. They are among its most important—and most demanding—elements. Avoiding those decisions may leave an organization largely unchanged while its strategy looks convincing on paper.

From ambition to organizational commitment

A credible strategy is not defined by the quality of its analysis or the attractiveness of its financial projections. It is defined by the clarity of its choices and by the organization’s willingness to act on their consequences.

Which markets will we prioritize—and which will we not? Which customers and channels deserve greater attention? Which products will we invest in, and which will we discontinue? What capabilities must be strengthened? And are we prepared to align our organization and talent with those choices?

The go-to-market model is where strategic ambition encounters commercial reality.

It is also where leaders discover whether they have developed a genuine strategy—or simply described a desirable future.

Questions worth asking

  • Is our go-to-market model clear enough to guide everyday commercial decisions?
  • Have we made genuine choices, or are we still trying to serve every market, customer and channel?
  • Does our organization possess the capabilities required to deliver the strategy?
  • Are we prepared to make the difficult decisions that execution inevitably demands?
