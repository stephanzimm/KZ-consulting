When a Leadership Team Becomes a Collection of Leaders

What happens when an executive loses sight of the team’s shared direction.

By Karel Zimmermann · 3 min read

Two responsibilities, one leadership team

A leadership team can be full of capable, hardworking people and still struggle to lead the business together.

Every executive has two responsibilities: to lead their own area well, and to share responsibility for the success of the wider business. These responsibilities will sometimes pull in different directions. That is precisely when the team must work as a team.

If members begin to act chiefly as advocates for the people and priorities closest to them, meetings can become negotiations between separate interests. Decisions slow down and an agreed direction becomes difficult to carry out.

When understanding becomes identification

In one of my leadership assignments, we acquired an attractive business. We appointed a member of the executive team as integration manager and moved him into the acquired company. His task was to help bring the businesses together, from people and ways of working to areas such as IT, Finance and Payroll.

He was intelligent, knowledgeable about the industry and skilled at coordinating across a business. We had good reason to believe he was right for the role.

Once on site, he got to know the people in the acquired business. Many saw the arrival of a larger company as a threat. That reaction was understandable. An integration manager needs to hear such concerns, earn trust and make sure the acquiring business understands what is at stake for the people joining it.

Over time, however, his approach shifted away from the integration we had agreed. He came to favour keeping the acquired company largely separate, with the two businesses exchanging information and good practices instead. Colleagues who had developed plans for integrating their areas were held back and asked to revisit them. Momentum slowed, and tension grew within the executive team.

His empathy was valuable. The difficulty was that, in trying to protect the people he now worked alongside, he had moved away from a direction he was also responsible for delivering.

A decision I should have made sooner

Eventually, I removed him from the integration role. We brought the relevant functional leaders directly into discussions with the acquired business and held regular town halls so people could understand the plans and raise their concerns. The integration work could move forward again.

Looking back, I should have addressed the change in direction sooner. We needed an open discussion about what he was hearing on site, whether our plans needed adjustment and which decisions still stood. Leaving the difference unresolved held back both the executive team and the people waiting for clarity in the acquired business.

The obligation to bring both views to the table

An executive working closely with an acquired business should challenge assumptions made before closing. The people on site may see risks the wider leadership team has missed. Listening to them can lead to a better integration.

But representing their concerns is only part of the executive’s job. The other part is to bring those concerns back to the team, help it make a decision and then act on that decision together.

That is the difference between a leadership team and a collection of leaders. Each member brings a perspective shaped by where they work and whom they lead.

Collective leadership requires them to use those perspectives in service of a shared direction.
